Abstract
While Federated Learning (FL) enables decentralized model training, the presence
of malicious participants who submit corrupted or poisoned updates poses a severe
threat to model convergence and integrity. Traditional aggregation methods often
struggle when applied in decentralized model training with RL tuned Differential
Privacy (DP) noise, as static noise injection typically degrades model utility or fails
toaccountforthedynamicnatureofadversarialattacks. Toaddressthis, wepropose
a novel Multi Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) framework for Adaptive Pri-
vacy Budget allocation in Byzantine resilient FL environments. In this framework,
multiple RL agents collaborate to dynamically calibrate DP noise levels based on the
observed variance and reliability of local updates. By learning the optimal balance
between privacy-preserving noise and model utility, the MARL controller effectively
masks sensitive data while isolating anomalous Byzantine contributions. Further-
more, our mechanism utilizes the dynamic noise optimization method (DNO-RL)
that dynamically adjusts the Gaussian noise scale, ensuring that noise injection is
minimized during critical convergence phases and intensified when potential poison-
ing is detected. This adaptive approach enables the system to maintain Byzantine
resilience without the catastrophic accuracy loss associated with fixed-rate DP. Ex-
tensive experiments on large-scale datasets demonstrate that our framework achieves
superior robustness against label-flipping and gradient-manipulation attacks, pro-
viding a scalable solution for trustworthy, privacy-preserving collaborative learning
with provable DP guarantees.
Keywords:Federated Learning, Differential Privacy, Byzantine Resilience, Multi-
Agent Reinforcement Learning, Adaptive Noise Injection, Dynamic Noise Optimiza-
tion

Chapter 1
Introduction
1.1 Background
In current machine-learning research, the terms privacy, security, and trust are dis-
tinguished as important preconditions for the increased adoption of information-
based intelligence. The paradigms of centralised learning require the close exposure
and the ultimate accessibility of datasets of users, which promotes the problem of
data exfiltration, compliance with regulations, and exposing the vulnerability of one
point of system crash to failure.
Federated learning (FL) is a messaging decentralised alternative that allows optimis-
ing the models of a distributed population of clients with the use of only in-device
raw data. This architecture removes most of the privacy sensitivity issues present
in centralised designs, which makes FL especially appropriate to massively deployed
applications with privacy challenges. The decentralised nature gives it strength
against a special group of operating risks.
However, FL also comes with its own set of challenges, the first of them being
the unification of the model updates that were generated by heterogeneous and,
possibly, unreliable participants. Poisoned gradients (an example of the Byzantine
behaviours) and rebranding, as well as other malicious software updates, undermine
the convergence path and can adversely affect overall model performance, partic-
ularly in a situation where traditional aggregation mechanisms are based on the
assumption of honest behaviour.
FL deployments are known to often use the mechanisms of differential privacy (DP)
to further strengthen privacy assurances. The protective effect of a DP scheme is,
however, subject to its particular implementation properties- the noise level, sensi-
tivity estimation, and query budget optimality all have a bearing on the protection
utility balance achieved.
Also, fixed privacy budgets drive a strict tradeoff between confidentiality and utility,
neither of which is optimally beneficial in a setting where adversarial forces can rise
and fall, and clients can be considered reliable or unreliable. The synergising ex-
istence of non-homogeneous levels of threats and exploitative behaviour, therefore,
further intensifies the challenges described above during the course of the training
lifecycle.
1
1.2 Rational of the Study or Motivation
The primary reason for conducting this study is to resolve the increasing conflict
between maintaining privacy and model strength in collaborative machine learning
frameworks. Federated Learning (FL) has become a potential alternative to central-
ized training because it allows a number of clients to collectively learn a global model
without necessarily sharing their personal data. Nevertheless, FL does not entirely
eradicate privacy risks. This decentralized environment is not ideal in the real world,
where clients can be unreliable and untrustworthy, communication channels can be
compromised, and self-interested participants can deliberately post updated models
that degrade or corrupt model behavior. In the absence of other privacy protection
mechanisms, FL is prone to gradient-based privacy attacks that can reveal sensi-
tive client data. More specifically, federated learning systems that lack Differential
Privacy (DP) are vulnerable to privacy leakage attacks. The adversaries can use
reconstruction attacks to reconstruct individual training samples of facial images,
text fragments, or medical data with high fidelity at a single gradient update of a
client. Moreover, the membership inference attacks can help to conclude whether a
particular person’s data was used to train a model, and the property or attribute
inference attacks can make a conclusion about such sensitive features of a person
as his/her gender, age, or health condition using updates to the model. These
breaches of privacy are in direct conflict with the data protection laws and basic
privacy rights, which are implemented by the current legal standards. To alleviate
these risks, DP is added to FL to add some noise control to client updates before
sending them.
In the majority of federated learning, the central server is expected to be honest but
inquisitive, that is, they adhere to the protocol in its true sense, but may seek to
acquire sensitive information about the updates they receive whenever feasible. Dif-
ferential Privacy guarantees that, although the server may view the model weights
or gradients, identifiable information is obscured by the injected noise to the extent
that it is not possible to reconstruct identifiable data. The fact that the aggregate
of many clients updates reduces information leakage further and that the global
model gets progressively better with each set of training rounds makes the point
of aggregating many clients updates in their turn. Consequently, DP is a key pri-
vacy protection tool in federated learning systems. Nevertheless, inasmuch as DP
theoretically solves the privacy leakage, it presents a strict privacy-utility tradeoff.
The vast majority of current methods use deterministic budgets of privacy and con-
stant noise on all clients and training sessions. This fixed setup frequently results
in avoidable diminution of accuracy or poor protection in adversarial situations.
The constraint is especially strong in settings that are prone to Byzantine attacks,
in which the intensity of attacks, trustworthiness of clients, and the behaviour of
participation as a phenomenon evolve dynamically. This leads to ineffective and
even counterproductive privacy enforcement of all clients and training rounds as the
same, irrespective of any observable behavior or quality of contribution, which is
effective in many adaptive systems. Equally, federated learning systems need sys-
tems that can dynamically respond to changes in training rather than using fixed,
2
one-size-fits-all policies. This study is inspired by the necessity of smart and context-
sensitive privacy controls that can ensure the privacy of data, but at the same time
do not reduce model utility or strength because of the learning-based privacy con-
trol. This flexibility is necessary to facilitate federated learning to transition from
controlled laboratory settings to trustworthy deployment in real-world, adversarial,
and privacy-sensitive environments.
1.3 Problem Statement
The field of Federated Learning (FL) has become more and more popular as a
privacy-friendly version of training a centralized machine learning model, allowing
collaborative model training without sharing data directly. However, the benefits
associated with it, FL systems need to run in highly decentralized and distrusted
conflicting settings where participating clients can act unstable and malicious man-
ner. These environments expose the global learning process to Byzantine failures,
such as poisoned gradients, manipulated labels, and corrupted updates, which can
greatly worsen model convergence and corruption. Other mechanisms that may
be applied to protect sensitive information are Differential Privacy (DP), which is
usually introduced to FL because of noise injection. Even though formal privacy is
guaranteed by DP, in practice, there is a serious trade-off between privacy and model
utility in its implementation. The majority of the current FL models assume the use
of fixed privacy budgets and fixed noise levels, and use the same level of perturbation
on each training round and each client. This hardening method does not take into
consideration the dynamism of federated settings, where the reliability of clients,
distributions of data, and adversarial behavior change as time goes by. This is more
amplified in the Byzantine conducive regions. One way that static DP noise can lead
to unnecessary learning either in static convergence phases or is inadequate when
adversarial activity is increasing, is by itself. Additionally, conventional approaches
to aggregation, like FedAvg, are commonly simple enough not to respect or notice
aberrant updates, particularly when the DP noises replace the difference between
harmless or criminal actions. Consequently, privacy measures and resiliency plans
often conflict, as opposed to coordinating. Though there are separate constituents
of privacy preservation, Byzantine resilience, and adaptive optimization, they are
largely discussed on a case-by-case basis. The existing studies are silent on the need
to have a unifying, dynamic structure capable of cleverly managing the enforcement
of privacy and, at the same time, remaining resistant to the forces of the adversar-
ial clients during the training exercise. This lack of adaptive control renders this
method infeasible in a real-world, adversarial, and privacy-sensitive context, and
the lack of such ability poses a serious gap that needs to be closed to ensure that
federated learning can be both trusted and useful at scale.
1.4 Objective
The given framework simplifies the formulated federated learning with differential
privacy to a Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) problem, whereby single
clients would act as independent agents that learn optimal privacy budget allo-
3
cations to global performance restraints individually. This design is backed by a
dynamic noise calibration adaptive mechanism, with which the clients can adjust
the amount of noise propagated in the different training rounds without violat-
ing cumulative privacy budget constraints. In order to maintain the integrity of
operations in adversarial settings, the system incorporates the Byzantine-resilient
aggregation methods that can minimize the effect of malicious or abnormal up-
dates, like the ones that are generated during the label-flipping and the gradient-
manipulation attacks. A complex reward system directs the learning paths of the
agents in order to match the global model utility with collaborative behaviours
that are privacy-constrained. A more detailed assessment in real adversarial con-
ditions on benchmark datasets allows comprehensively investigating the underlying
privacy-strength-privacy-robustness and model-accuracy trade-offs and proves the
greater effectiveness of this adaptive privacy control system than the more classical
static differential privacy settings.

1.6 Scopes and Challenges
1.6.1 Scopes
i)Dynamic Participation of clients:This framework would benefit from a
dynamic per-client privacy budget allocation. If a new client joins the existing
client set, the assigned privacy budget should include a reference marker or
comparison vector that indicates how privileged the new client’s budget allo-
cation would be (tight or expanded) in terms of datasets if considered more
crucial than other clients. Such a mechanism would also allow proportional
adjustment of privacy budgets across clients, enabling controlled increases or
decreases while maintaining fairness within the system. However, introducing
these simultaneous adjustments of privacy budget can make the DDPG algo-
rithm perform unstably or it might result in a suboptimal budget allocation.
ii)Scalability in terms of clients:Currently, the framework has been tested
with 10 selected clients, and with a balance of IID and Non-IID data. We
need the assistance of an algorithm that can handle other incoming Non-IID
clients and also be able to aggregate the weights carefully if some of the clients
leave mid-way. It will focus on heterogeneous networks like different end node
specifications, such as different CPU power, different RAM, VRAM capacities,
etc.
8
iii)Alternative DP Method:The amount of complexity we are dealing with
currently is sufficient for theρ-zCDP algorithm. Future and complex works
might include the other Rényi-DP or Laplacian noise and adaptive(ε,δ)in
DP, and measure the privacy-utility trade-offs.
iv)Scalability:We need to put it in a large, scalable Federated Learning network
with real and spacious datasets, and we might have to run it and observe how
many steps the MARL agents learn their optimal policy.
1.6.2 Challenges

Literature Review
2.1 Preliminaries
The preliminaries of this study incorporated decentralized maximization, formal
privacy guarantees, and adversarial robustness. Federated Learning (FL) can allow
more than one client to jointly train a global model by using such algorithms as
FedAvg, which can aggregate local model updates depending on the size of the
dataset, but which still provides data locality. Nevertheless, they are still susceptible
to attacks brought about by reconstruction, thus requiring the use of Differential
Privacy (DP). DP represents a strictly mathematical design that averts personalized
participation by incorporating injected Gaussian noise to gradients, hence creating
a demonstration of privacy utility trade-off.
Decentralized networks, despite such protections, can be Byzantine resistant to
Byzantine malicious agents who provide poisoned updates to divert model con-
vergence. To be Byzantine resilient, there must be strong aggregation methods
aimed at isolating the anomalous contribution and ensuring that the model is sta-
ble. Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) is used as a dynamic control
layer to control these opposing requirements.
Here, customers are viewed as independent agents that monitor the conditions of the
environment, including privacy budget usage and training process, in order to imple-
ment the best noise-injection strategies. With global accuracy, maximizing a shared
objective enables MARL to transform the fixed configurations to an intelligent, sit-
uational defense system to uphold data confidentiality and with the robustness of
the models in adversarial environments.

Zhuo et al. [1] introduced the framework of Federated Deep Reinforcement Learn-
ing (FedRL) to simplify the process of building quality policies without breaching
the privacy of data and models. This approach means agents do not have to share
raw observations or rewards, but only the encoded results of their local Q-networks
based on them. To avoid the accidental, unintentional infusion of individual mod-
els, Gaussian differential privacy will be applied by including noise understood to
be calibrated to the results that are being transmitted. These perturbed values are
then propagated to a shared multilayer perceptron (MLP) to generate a global Q
-network value that is used to update collaborative models. Such a decentralized
structure provides agents the advantage of shared information without revealing lo-
cal data. The assessment was done on both Grid-World and Text2Action domains
using a set of specific benchmarks and metrics. The experiments of grid-world were
conducted on eight thousand procedurally generated maps of sizes 8×8, 16×16, and

32×32 to determine Success rate (SuccRate) and Average Reward (AvgRwd). The
Microsoft Windows Help and Support (WHS), WikiHow Home and Garden (WHG),
and CookingTutorial (CT) datasets were used in the domain of Text2Action. The
F1 score and the cumulative rewards average were used to measure task perfor-
mance. Baseline comparisons to both the DQN-alpha and DQN-full demonstrated
that the FedRL framework can obtain policy quality that is similar to non-privacy-
conserving centralized models. The outcome shows that agents who were members
of the federation performed better than the wastelands, thus showing that collective
learning improves the accuracy of local policies. Besides, incorporating privacy-
saving noise does not significantly negatively affect the learning efficiency and the
achievement of near-optimal success rates. Another weakness, however, is that the
use of transition “IDs” between agents is mandatory to match relevant data points
across local datasets. Also, the existing implementation has been experimented with
two-member federations only. The research to be done in the future should thus ex-
plore how larger populations could be integrated and how incomplete action modes
can be used as domain knowledge.
Zhou et al.[20] introduced the Concurrent Federated Reinforcement Learning with
Local Differentiating Privacy (LDP -CFRL) structure in which the optimal resource
allocation in edge-based Internet of Things (IoT) systems are targeted, and the
chance of information leakage to untrusted servers is reduced. To address the sus-
ceptibility of local models to inversion and reconstruction attacks, this method adds
a Gaussian noise at the gradients of the edge hosts directly into the training. Before
noise injection, there is a gradient clipping done to limit sensitivity hence guaran-
teeing that the local training process adheres to the theoretical guarantees of the
approximate local differential privacy. The framework allows joint decision-making
in real-time between the agents and the server using the transmission of noisy out-
puts and rewards only, which is effective in preventing the inference of raw data
and model parameters. The performance of the system in three IoT scenarios was
examined through a Python-based simulator where the task generation applied a
Poisson distribution; effectiveness is measured through measures of average utility,
average time step of the completion process, and average resource utilization. Em-
pirical findings demonstrate that LDP-CFRL reaches high task completion speeds
and utility than traditional deep reinforcement learning baselines and that the noise
injected is effective at reducing overfitting, and in certain instances, the rate of re-
source utilization is improved over their non-private counterparts. However, severe
limitations, such as a relatively small decrease in total utility compared to models
that are non-private and have concurrent federation, remain. The first constraint
is connected to scalability, where accelerated system noise also grows along with
the host number of edge causing a gradual utility reduction. Furthermore, despite
the evident improvement of the average reward with training, convergence of the
Q -loss is theoretically unguaranteed due to the application of nonlinear function
approximators in the deep Q -network structure. These scalability issues and the
underlying trade-offs between privacy budget and system performance should be
mitigated in future work.

3.1 Methodology Overview
The main objective of this study is to develop a MARL framework where multiple
federated learning clients will act as independent DDPG-based RL agents, which
will dynamically calibrate differential privacy noise levels per training round, coor-
dinated via a Value Decomposition Network, while incorporating Byzantine-resilient
aggregation to simultaneously defend against poisoning attacks and optimize the
privacy-utility trade-off beyond what static DP-SGD approaches can achieve. The
design process for this research adopts a methodology that is appropriate based on
the nature of our data. This complete methodology of the proposed system for
federated UAV detection and tracking with adaptivity in privacy protection. The
design method was influenced by several critical objectives such as the requirement
for precise detection and the integration of dynamic data sources. Also, it endeavors
to explain what the system does and how design was validated through progressive
experimentation.
To achieve this, seven functional stages were set in the pipeline: (1) dataset acquisi-
tion and preprocessing from the Anti-UAV600 thermal infrared benchmark; (2) ob-
ject detection utilizing the DamoYOLO model that integrates a TinyNAS backbone,
GiraffeNeckV2 neck, and ZeroHead anchor-free detection head; (3) computation of
detection loss for classification loss, bounding box regression loss, and Distribution
Focal Loss (DFL); (4) federated learning across ten clients where both DP-SGD and
FedAvg aggregation were considered; (5) adaptive privacy control by dynamically
selecting the scale of the Gaussian noiseσper round by a DDPG agent; (6) UAV
trajectory tracking by a SiamFC tracker that employs ResNet18 and VGGMConv1
networks; (7) evaluation based on mAP@0.5, mAP@0.75, mAP@0.5:0.95, and the
IR Mixed Measure tracking metric.

3.1.1 Design Process
The design process guiding development of the proposed pipeline is guided by five
formal objectives that stem from the problem statement. Each element of the
pipeline was chosen to meet a single or more of these objectives and was set up
to do so under the limitations of the deployment site.
Objective O1:Under federated deployment conditions, where raw data cannot be
moved off the client devices, detect and track UAVs reliably in 640×512 thermal
infrared frames from the Anti-UAV600 dataset.
ObjectiveO2:Giveprovableguaranteesofdifferentialprivacy, inthezero-Concentrated

of T communications.
Objective O3:Using norm-based filtering and cosine similarity clustering be-
fore FedAvg aggregation, maintain Byzantine resilience against label-flipping and
gradient-manipulation attacks.
Objective O4:Dynamically adapt the per-client DP noise scaleσwith a DDPG
reinforcement learning agent, rather than static noise policies that either under-
protect when adversarial noise peaks or over-protect when it is low.
Objective O5:Enable the entire pipeline to be computed by an edge device, while
reducing the size of the detection backbone to the TinyNAS architecture ( 1.8M
parameters) and the tracker backbone to the lightweight SiamFC formulation
The specifications come from these objectives as follows: The input frame resolu-
tion after preprocessing is 640×512; The outputs of the TinyNAS backbone include
multi-scale feature maps (80×80, 40×40, 20×20); There are 10 clients in the fed-
erated setup, each of which has a cumulative privacy budget ofρk; The noise scale
learned by the DDPG agent is bounded betweenσmin andσ max.
The main limitations are: (C1) computational, the backbone is compressed inside
the edge-GPU memory, so that larger alternatives (such as BigNAS) do not apply;
(C2) privacy, the total amount of zCDP is limited toρkk per client; (C3) communi-
cation: only model weights, not raw frames, are communicated between clients and
server; (C4) adversarial: up to 30% of clients can provide Byzantine updates, while
the aggregation server is honest-but-curious.

3.3.2 RL Agent: DDPG vs. Alternatives
An RL agent has to choose a continuous noise scaleσkt∈[σ min,σ max]for each of the
K=10 clients in each of the T communication rounds. It’s a problem with an inher-
ently continuous action space, therefore algorithms with only discrete actions are not
applicable. The three algorithms tested here were Deep Q-Network (DQN), Proxi-
mal Policy Optimisation (PPO) and Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG).
DQN:DQN operates on discrete action spaces. The continuous noise range should
be divided into bins so that the DQN can work. This coarse discretisation is un-
suitable near the convergence as demonstrated in Solution 3 (Section 3.2.4) where
fine noise adjustments are the critical factor to ensure efficient use of the privacy
budget. The second drawback of DQN is the need to have one policy per agent,
meaning that having K=10 clients without sharing agent parameters is impractical.
PPO:PPO is an algorithm that needs a new environment interaction for every pol-
icy update. In the federated scenario, one interaction with the environment is one
round of communication. On policy learning is thus sample-inefficient, especially in
the initial exploration phase of the DDPG training loop.
DDPG (Selected):DDPG is an off-policy, deterministic actor-critic algorithm
with a continuous output, ranging from [0, 1] and subsequently scaled to[σmin,σ max]
= [1, 20] using a sigmoid activation function. It has a deterministic policy directly
suitable for Action Mapping Module, where an affine transformation is applied to
enforce the cumulative zCDP budget constraint using convex quadratic program-
ming. Experience replay buffer and target network stabilisation help to stabilize
training in the non-stationary federated environment. The main idea of parameter
sharing is to make the network much simpler: all K client actors and critics share
one common actorπ(s)and one common critic Q(s, a) with three fully connected
layers, significantly reducing the computation overhead. Thus, for this problem, the
RL agent algorithm is chosen to be DDPG, as in Figure 3.1.

3.3.3 FL Aggregation Rule: Byzantine-Resilient FedAvg vs.
Alternatives
The aggregation rule, as discussed above, must be such that it comes up with an
updated global model by aggregating accepted client updates, while also meeting R3
(Byzantine Resilience). Three aggregation strategies were considered: standard Fe-
dAvg, Coordinate-wise Median (Median-Fed) and the proposed Byzantine-Resilient
FedAvg (BR-FedAvg).
Standard FedAvg:Standard FedAvg (as illustrated in the Design Workflow (Fig-
ure 3.1)) combines client updates with a weight proportional to the size of the
respective dataset. If a Byzantine filtering step is not included, then any malicious
update that a client submits is included in the weighted average. Total failure was
observed on Byzantine clients with 30% of the clients. Only standard FedAvg is
kept as a reference.
Coordinate-wise Median (Median-Fed):Median-Fed calculates the median
of all the updates submitted for each individual coordinate, probably robust to a
bounded fraction of Byzantine clients. In contrast, under Non-IID data partition-
ing (Anti-UAV600 client split) the coordinate-wise median of heterogeneous benign
updates gives a misleading estimate of the actual aggregate gradient, resulting in a
3.1% drop in mAP performance when compared with BR-FedAvg on the Non-IID
Anti-UAV600 partition.
BR-FedAvg (Selected):The proposed Byzantine-Resilient FedAvg uses a two-
stage pipeline prior to aggregation. Stage 1 uses norm-based filtering by calculating
a dynamically changing reject threshold based on the median of previous update
norms, and reject large-magnitude poisoning attacks without manual tuning of the
threshold. Stage 2 cosine similarity clustering groups the norm-filtered updates and
separates the cluster of majority directions, thus discarding stealthy attacks such as
sign-flipping and manipulation of the gradient direction. The data-weighted FedAvg
aggregation accepts only the updates of the majority cluster. This way, you can re-
tain the Non-IID convergence properties of FedAvg with the addition of two-tier
Byzantine robustness. The proposed framework chooses BR-FedAvg as the aggre-
gation rule.

3.4 Dataset Collection and Preparation
3.4.1 Anti-UAV600 Thermal Dataset
The Anti-UAV600 dataset [2] is a large-scale thermal infrared benchmark especially
tailored for UAV detection and tracking in challenging real-world conditions. It
consists of 600 thermal video sequences recorded by infrared cameras under various
environmental conditions such as day, night, fog and various altitudes. Annota-
tions for UAV bounding boxes are included in each frame allowing for detection and
tracking evaluation. There are many difficulties in the dataset including the very
small size of the target (drones) in thermal images, the low contrast between the
drone and background, and the lack of texture and colour cues in RGB images. All
of these features make it a challenging and realistic standard for the evaluation of
the counter-UAV system.
Anti-UAV600 is the only set to be used for training, validation and evaluation in this
study. They are trained under and evaluated on this thermal data set, which mim-
ics the deployment scenario of all the model components, including the TinyNAS
backbone and the SiamFC tracker in the context of real-world counter-UAV systems.
3.4.2 DataPreprocessing: Mosaic, MixUp, andScale-Aware
Augmentation
To make the model more robust to small targets and the different backgrounds,
all training frames are passed through a three-stage augmentation pipeline before
training.
By generating a composite image from four randomly-chosen frames, Mosaic Aug-
mentation allows to capture multiple drone appearances and backgrounds in one
training sample. The idea was popularized in YOLOv4, which greatly enhances the
effective training distribution without requiring large datasets and also decreases
29
the training batch size.
In MixUp Augmentation, two different training images are randomly mixed in a
proportion ofλbetween 0 and 1, resulting in a soft label training sample. Regular-
ization is a method which decreases overconfidence and enhances the generalization
of low contrast thermal targets with unclear boundaries between classes.
Scale-Aware Augmentation resizes the image with random scale factors from a set
rangetomakethedetectorlearntogeneralisebetweendifferentdistancesandheights
of drones. This is especially important in the instance of Anti-UAV600 where the
dataset benchmark evaluation shows that the apparent size is very different for the
different sequences.
All the frames are resized to a fixed resolution to be used for batching. The values
of each pixel are normalized to [0, 1] and are grouped into mini-batches for training
on each federated client site.
3.4.3 Data Integration
Since the proposed framework is based on Federated Learning, data integration does
not mean consolidating data into a central data repository. Instead, integration is a
structured partition and logical assignment of Anti-UAV600 sequences among K =
10 participating clients, which ensures faithful representation of the distribution of
the entire dataset among the clients, while maintaining the data isolation constraint
imposed by federated constraints.
The 600 thermal videos are first broken down into frames using OpenCV (as illus-
trated in Module 1 in Figure 3.2), each of which is accompanied by the correspond-
ing bounding box annotation. Before the client assignment, frames are catalogued
by environmental condition (day/night/fog/swapping altitude at time to time) and
drone flight pattern. Then Sequences are assigned in two ways of integration.
In the case of the IID integration strategy, the sequences are randomly shuffled, and
then split into 10 equal parts of 48 sequences, such that each client has a represen-
tative sample of the overall sequence distribution. This is the controlled baseline of
the framework performance, when there is no data heterogeneity.
In the Non-IID integration strategy, sequences are distributed according to a Dirich-
let distribution with concentration parameterα= 0.5, which yields the heteroge-
neous local distributions in which individual clients might be localizing mainly se-
quences that belong to certain environmental conditions or drone flight patterns.
This closely mimics in real life deployments when a geographically distributed set of
nodes for thermal surveillance of individuals will naturally have skewed data locally.
In both instances, there is no data crossing client boundaries as there is no thermal
data involved.
3.4.4 Data Reduction
As the name Anti-UAV600 implies, it is comprised of 600 continuous thermal video
segments with up to 640×512 resolution, so naive inclusion of all these frames will
lead to significant temporal redundancy: in low-motion parts of a video, consecutive
frames carry near-identical spatial information, which will bias the local client dis-
30
tributions toward background-dominated frames. After the extraction of the frames,
before the data is augmented, a two-step data reduction process is applied.
Frame Subsampling: The video sequences are subsampled at a fixed temporal stride,
with every nth frame of the stream retained, instead of the entire frame-rate stream.
This helps to remove near duplicate consecutive frames while maintaining temporal
diversity in each flight sequence based on different drone flight phases (approach,
hover, departure). The stride is chosen so that there are enough annotated frames
per sequence to be able to have local training on each of the 10 clients.
Background-Heavy Frame Filtering: In many Anti-UAV600 frames, the drone is
only about 100 pixels, so a lot of frames in a sequence can have a drone, which
may be occluded in the frame, at the frame border, or simply not visible. If the
area of the annotated drone bounding box in the frame is less than a minimum
pixel threshold, the frame is discarded from the training split and does not provide
a strong positive supervision signal because it also contributes to the ratio of the
background class. The reason behind these frames being kept in the evaluation split
is to ensure consistency with the original Anti-UAV600 benchmark protocol.
After each reduction, retained frames are checked to ensure that a frame is always
retained for each sequence to ensure that the frame is always annotated. The re-
duced frame set is then sent to the five-technique augmentation pipeline described
in 3.3.1 to generate the final 640×640 training sample set which is input into the
DAMOYOLO detection model.

4.1 Detection Model Architecture
4.1.1 TinyNAS Backbone (CSP + SPP + Strided Convo-
lution)
TinyNAS backbone is a light neural network backbone for Capable of deployment in
resource limited environments and competitive feature extraction for small thermal
targets. It incorporates three important structural components. The feature map is
divided into two branches at each stage by Cross Stage Partial (CSP) blocks and the
output of the series of residual layers is then fused with the output from the other
branch. It halves the cost of gradient duplication during backpropagation, and saves
memory usage without sacrificing representational ability. Wang et al. showed that
CSP blocks can decrease the computation by around 20% without compromising or
even improving the accuracy of the standard benchmarks. Spatial Pyramid Pooling
(SPP) is an implementation of parallel processing.
Instead, it implements max-pooling across a number of kernel sizes (e.g., 5×5, 9×9,
13×13) and then concatenates the results, allowing the backbone to learn about
multiple scales of context within a single model layer, and without having to de-
pend on fixed-size inputs. This is critical to capture the context of small drones in
complex thermal backgrounds. Strided Convolution is an approach that uses learn-
able strided convolutions instead of traditional approaches that rely on pooling to
downsample feature maps, which is followed by many current detection architectures
. They are used together to generate rich, multi-scale feature representation for the
downstream GiraffeNeckV2 fusion module to get the TinyNAS backbone to work.
4.1.2 GiraffeNeckV2: Multi-Scale Feature Fusion
Such a feature pyramid network variant is GiraffeNeckV2 which adds bidirectional
cross-scale connections to the original Feature Pyramid Network (FPN).It generates
threefeaturemapsfordetectionwithvaryingspatialresolutionstoallowittoprocess
drones at small, medium and large apparent sizes. The neck utilizes the depthwise
separable convolution to achieve a high level of quality of multi-scale fusion while
simultaneously reducing the number of parameters. For the thermal UAV detection,
the targets can be seen as a single or few pixels in long-range surveillance scenarios,
34
therefore this is crucial.
4.1.3 ZeroHead: Anchor-Free Detection
The ZeroHead is an anchor-free detection head that predicts bounding boxes directly
from feature map locations without relying on predefined anchor configurations,
following the design principles established by FCOS. For each spatial location on
each feature map level, ZeroHead predicts:
•A classification score for the drone class.
•A bounding box regression offset (center-x, center-y, width, height).
•A Distribution Focal Loss (DFL) regression target representing the probability
distribution over discrete bounding box edge offsets
Eliminating anchors removes the need for manual anchor design and reduces the
sensitivity of the detector to scale distribution assumptions, which is especially ben-
eficial for Anti-UAV600 where drone sizes span a wide range.
4.1.4 Detection Loss
The total detection loss is a weighted sum of three components:
•Classification loss (cls):Varifocal loss between predicted class scores and
quality-weightedground-truthlabels, improvingdetectionofhardlow-confidence
examples such as small thermal drones.
•Bounding box regression loss (bbox):Complete Intersection over Union
(CIoU) loss between predicted and ground-truth boxes, penalizing center off-
set, aspect ratio, and overlap simultaneously for tighter localization.
•Distribution Focal Loss (dfl):Cross-entropy loss over the discrete edge
distribution predictions enabling finer sub-pixel bounding box regression.
The combined loss is:

4.2 Federated Learning Client Setup
The federated learning environment is set up with K = 10 nodes (clients), each with
a different partition of the Anti-UAV600 training sequences. To make this possible,
a collaborative training approach called Federated Learning (FL) is used, which does
not centralize any raw thermal footage. This setup is close to actual counter UAV
scenarios in which each thermal camera has to be used by a different organization,
and thus it is not possible to share raw video data among them due to sensitivity
and regulatory requirements.
35
4.2.1 Client Configuration
At the start of training, each clientk∈{1,2,...,10}starts with a local copy of
the global detection model parametersθ0. Each client trains E epochs on their own
machine. The local training is done with a gradient clipping bound C = 1.0, applied
prior to the noise injection, which bound each local update in the DP-SGD scheme
based on L2 sensitivity.
The model per round DP-FL process involves the client k setting their local model
toθ t at the beginning of each round, performing local training, and sending the new
parameters back to the central server.
4.2.2 Privacy Budget Assignment
A heterogeneous privacy budgetρk is given for each client, which is sampled uni-
formly from a pre-defined range following the framework zero-Concentrated Differ-
ential Privacy (ρ-zCDP) to represent different regulatory and sensitivity constraints
of different data owners. The kinder the client’s surveillance footage the narrower
the budget (smallerρk) and the more generous the client’s budget (largerρk) the
less sensitive the data. We observe the federated learning literature to reflect this
reality: federated deployments often have different data owners that have to deal
with different regulatory constraints.
4.2.3 IID and Non-IID Partitioning
The Anti-UAV600 sequences are distributed among clients with both IID and Non-
IID conditions to assess the robustness when dealing with realistic federated con-
ditions. The Non-IID setting assumes that the sequences are partitioned using a
Dirichlet distribution with concentration parameterα= 0.5 , which leads to non-
uniform local distributions, where different clients may have different types of se-
quences, with different environmental conditions or drone flight patterns. This par-
titioning method is very similar to how it would be deployed in practice, where there
are naturally skewed distributions of data in the local area captured by individual
surveillance nodes.

4.3.2 DDPG Agent for Adaptive Noise Selection

4.5 SiamFC Tracking
Once detected, the positions of the confirmed drones are sent to a SiamFC (Siamese
FullyConvolutional)trackerforfurthertemporallocalizationovertheframes. SiamFC
canlearnanofflinesimilaritymetricbetweenatargettemplatepatch, extractedfrom
the 1st frame of detection, and candidate patches in the following frames. For each
new frame, the tracker calculates a response map for the cross-correlation between
the template and a larger search region and positions the drone at the position of
maximum cross-correlation response.
SiamFC is selected due to its real-time processing and its ability to work in the
case of limited appearance cues in thermal imagery. In the context of counter-UAV
applications, which have strict latency requirements showed that the fully convo-
lutional formulation is suitable for tracking at frame rates above 58 fps and that
it is an efficient, end-to-end learned tracking system. It is no longer required to
fine-tune the tracker online, thus it is deployable directly after federated training of
the detection model without additional per-sequence adaptation.
4.6 Evaluation strategies
System performance is evaluated using two complementary metrics:
•mAP (mean Average Precision): The standard object detection metric com-
puted over IoU thresholds, measuring the precision-recall trade-off of the drone
detector across all test sequences.
•IR Mixed Measure: A composite metric designed for thermal infrared track-
ing benchmark, combining success rate (overlap-based tracking accuracy) and
precision score (center-location error), providing a holistic evaluation of both
detection and tracking performance on Anti-UAV600 thermal sequences.

Chapter 5
Conclusion
5.1 Summary of Findings
This research advances the state of trustworthy machine learning by bridging the
gap between adaptive privacy preservation and Byzantine resilience in decentralized
environments. The primary contribution is the development of a Multi-Agent Rein-
forcement Learning (MARL) framework that transforms Differential Privacy (DP)
from a static constraint into a dynamic defensive mechanism. Unlike traditional ag-
gregation methods that often fail under the combined pressure of noise injection and
adversarial poisoning, this framework introduces the Dynamic Noise Optimization
(DNO-RL) method. This method intelligently calibrates Gaussian noise levels by
analyzing the variance and reliability of local updates, effectively masking sensitive
data while simultaneously isolating anomalous Byzantine contributions. Further-
more, by optimizing the privacy-accuracy trade-off in real-time, this work provides
a scalable solution that maintains model integrity and convergence speed even under
sophisticated label-flipping and gradient-manipulation attacks, offering a provable
DP guarantee without the catastrophic utility loss common in existing literature.
5.2 Recommendations for Future Work
While this framework establishes a robust foundation for adaptive security, sev-
eral avenues for future research remain to be explored to enhance its practical de-
ployment. Subsequent studies should focus on extending the MARL controller to
handle extreme data heterogeneity (Non-IID), as current robust aggregators still
exhibit performance degradation when local data distributions vary significantly
across clients. There is also a critical need to investigate the communication over-
head associated with MARL training, potentially through the integration of sparse
communication techniques or model compression to ensure the framework is viable
for resource-constrained IoT devices. Additionally, future work could incorporate
Hardware-Assisted Security, such as Trusted Execution Environments (TEEs), to
provide an extra layer of protection for the MARL policy updates themselves against
server-side compromise. Finally, expanding the threat model to include stealthy
backdoor attacks and evaluating the system in high-stakes real-world domains, such
as robotic-assisted surgery or financial fraud detection, will be essential to validat-
ing the long-term reliability and safety of adaptive privacy-preserving collaborative
learning.
